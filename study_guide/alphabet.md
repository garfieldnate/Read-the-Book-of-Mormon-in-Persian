# Persian Alphabet (الفبا)

[TOC]

## The name

Iranians call their writing system **الفبا** (_alifbā_) — a compound of the names of the first two letters, **الف** (_alef_) and **ب** (_be_), exactly as "alphabet" combines Greek _alpha_ and _beta_.

## Overview

- **32 letters** (Arabic has 28; the four letters unique to Persian are پ _pe_, چ _če_, ژ _zhe_, گ _gāf_)
- Written **right-to-left**; words are separated by spaces
- An ***abjad*** (consonantal alphabet): short vowels are normally unwritten in everyday text; the three long vowels ā, ī, ū are written with letter-symbols
- Most letters join to the adjacent letter within a word; **seven do not** (the "non-connecting" letters — see below)

**On spelling collisions**: Persian spelling preserves Arabic distinctions that pronunciation has collapsed. There are effectively five ways to spell /s/ (ث س ص), four ways to spell /z/ (ذ ز ض ظ), and two ways to spell /t/ (ت ط) and /h/ (ح ه). The correct spelling must be memorised per word; no phonetic rule predicts it. ## Connecting and non-connecting letters

Seven letters never extend a connecting stroke to the **left** (toward the next letter): **ا د ذ ر ز ژ و**. When one appears in the middle of a word, the letters after it begin a new connected run — the word splits into clusters. Compare:

- بستن (_bastan_ "to close"): ب connects to س, which connects to ت, which connects to ن — one unbroken chain
- درس (_dars_ "lesson"): both د and ر are non-connectors, so the word is three separate shapes: **د** + **ر** + **س**
- نوروز (_nowruz_ "New Year"): ن + و (break) + ر (break) + و (break) + ز — four separate shapes joined only by proximity and spacing

## Common ligatures

The standard print style used throughout these guides — and in most modern Persian books, newspapers, and digital text — is **Naskh** (نسخ, _naskh_): upright letterforms with even stroke weight, suited to running text on screen and paper. Within Naskh, most letter combinations are simply the four positional forms joined edge-to-edge. Two ligatures, however, are nearly universal across all Arabic-script fonts:

### لا — lam-alef

Whenever ل (lam) is immediately followed by ا — or any alef variant: آ أ إ — the two always fuse into a single combined glyph. This is the one ligature required by Unicode for compliant rendering. You will see it constantly: لا (_lā_) "no / not", کلاس (_kelās_) "class", دلایل (_dalāyel_) "reasons".

### الله — the name of God

The word الله (_allāh_) is rendered differently depending on the font. In dedicated calligraphic fonts, the two lams fuse into a single tall stroke with the shadda above and the final he hangs as a downward loop — the classical ligature. Most general-purpose Naskh system fonts render the letters more plainly: alef + two lams (the second carrying the shadda) + he, without the fusion. The Nastaliq column below shows the classical form:

|---|:---:|:---:|
| الله (_allāh_) | الله | الله |

## Diacritics

Short vowel marks are called **حرکت** (_harakat_, "movements"). They are absent from everyday adult text but appear in children's books, the Quran, poetry, and wherever ambiguity must be resolved.

### Short vowels

| Persian name | Mark | Translit. | Pronunciation |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| فتحه / زَبَر (_fat-he / zabar_) | `هَ` | `a` | short /a/ |
| کسره / زیر (_kasr-e / zīr_) | `هِ` | `e` | short /e/ |
| ضمه / پیش (_zamme / piš_) | `هُ` | `o` | short /o/ |

### Other marks

| Persian name | Mark | Function |
|---|:---:|---|
| سکون / جزم (_sokun / jazm_) | `هْ` | explicitly marks no vowel — the syllable is closed here |
| تشدید (_tašdid_) | `هّ` | gemination — the consonant is doubled or held |
| مَدّ (_madd_) | `آ` | long ā: alef with this superscript at word-initial position: آب (_āb_) "water", آمد (_āmad_) "came" |

## Hamza (همزه)

Hamza (ء) marks a glottal stop or syllable boundary, borrowed from Arabic. It is written on a **کرسی** (_korsī_, "chair") — a carrier letter whose shape depends on the surrounding vowel context:

| Form | کرسی (_korsī_, "chair") | When used | Example |
|:---:|---|---|---|
| ئ | یاء (ye) | adjacent to /e/ or /i/ | رئیس (_raʾīs_) "president" |
| ؤ | واو (vāv) | adjacent to /o/ or /u/ | مؤمن (_moʾmen_) "believer" |
| ء | bare — no کرسی | word-finally, or after a long vowel | جزء (_jozʾ_) "part" |

The Arabic forms أ (hamza above alef) and إ (hamza below alef) do not appear in standard Persian writing. Word-initial vowels are written with plain ا or آ — never with hamzated alef.

## Irregularities from Arabic

### الف مقصوره — "shortened alef"

Arabic final ye normally retains the lower two dots (ي), unlike Persian, which removes the dots in final position. However, in some circumstances a word-final long /ā/ is written in Arabic using a dotless variant of ye, ى. When such an imported word appears in Persian text, it is easy to accidentally pronounce it as an ending -i because of the ye shape. Many names of Biblical figures are borrowed from Arabic and spelled with this dotless ye, so the correct pronunciation must be memorised per word, e.g.:

| Arabic spelling | Translit. | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| عیسى | _ʿīsā_ | Jesus |
| موسى | _Mūsā_ | Moses |
| مستثنى | _mostasnā_ | excluded |

### Round te (تای گرد / تاء مربوطة)

ة — called تای گرد (_te-ye gerd_, "round te") in Persian and تاء مربوطة (_tā' marbūṭa_, "tied te") in Arabic — looks like a ه with two dots above it. It is word-final only and occurs in Arabic loanword constructions. Pronounce it _-at_. It almost always carries a tanwin termination (see below). In modern Persian typography it is often replaced by ت, so you may see the same word spelled either way:

- ة (_-at_): رحمة (_raḥmat_) "mercy", نعمة (_neʿmat_) "blessing"
- ت (_-at_): رحمت (_raḥmat_) "mercy", نعمت (_neʿmat_) "blessing"

### تنوین — tanvin

Tanvin (تنوین, _tanvin_) is the Arabic adverbial ending, pronounced _-an_. In Persian it survives only as a frozen suffix on a small set of Arabic loanwords used as adverbs. It is written as a double-fatḥa mark "carried" on a final alef (اً):

- مثلاً (_masalan_) "for example"
- واقعاً (_vāqeʿan_) "truly, really"

When tanvin falls on a round te (ة), it does not take the alef carrier — the mark sits directly on the ة:

- نسبةً (_nesbatan_) "relatively"
- دفعةً (_dafʿatan_) "all at once"

Many writers of modern Persian treat the round te as a regular ت and add the alef, so both spellings are seen:

- نسبتاً alongside نسبةً
- دفعتاً alongside دفعةً

## Nastaliq script (خط نستعلیق)

All text in these study guides is set in **Naskh** (نسخ) — the upright, horizontally-aligned style used in most digital fonts and modern print. But the dominant traditional script for Persian literature, poetry, formal correspondence, and calligraphy is **Nastaliq** (نستعلیق, _nastaʿlīq_). The name is a blend of _naskh_ and _taʿlīq_ (a predecessor style). You will encounter Nastaliq in older printed books, illuminated manuscripts, Iranian newspapers, handwritten Persian, and decorative contexts everywhere.

### How Nastaliq differs from Naskh

| Feature | Naskh | Nastaliq |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Horizontal | Diagonal — each word cascades lower than the one to its right |
| Stroke weight | Even throughout | Thick on downstrokes, thin on horizontal strokes |
| Word shape | Flat, linear | Words "fall" in steps; text block has a staircase profile |
| Dot placement | Immediately above or below the letter | Often stacked vertically; repositioned for visual balance |
| Ligature count | ~100–200 contextual forms | 1,000–10,000+ depending on the font |

The cascading baseline is the most immediately striking feature for newcomers: a long line of Persian poetry in Nastaliq does not sit flat; it descends in steps from right to left, with each phrase lower than the last.

### Nastaliq ligatures

Beyond the four standard positional forms that any Arabic-script font implements, Nastaliq mandates hundreds of additional **ligature substitutions** where specific letter sequences fuse into glyphs that look nothing like the sum of their parts. The tables below show the same text in Naskh and Nastaliq. This is just a small sample of the most common ligatures; a full Nastaliq font may have thousands of these substitutions.

**کاف / گاف with following letter** — in Nastaliq, kāf or gāf shrinks into a shallow diagonal and the following letter rises above it, nestled against the stroke:

| <span id="nastaliq-lig-kaf">Sequence</span> | Naskh | Nastaliq |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| kāf + nun | کن | کن |
| gāf + nun | گن | گن |
| kāf + te | کت | کت |
| kāf + alef | کا | کا |

**Final ی sequences** — the ye sweeps down and to the left; the preceding letter rotates to align with it:

| <span id="nastaliq-lig-ye">Sequence</span> | Naskh | Nastaliq |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| be + ye (بی, _bi-_) | بی | بی |
| mim + ye (می, _mi-_) | می | می |
| pe + ye (پی, _pay_) | پی | پی |

**Common short words** — whole words frequently collapse to a single distinctive glyph:

| <span id="nastaliq-lig-words">Word</span> | Naskh | Nastaliq |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| می (_mi-_, verbal prefix) | می | می |
| که (_ke_, "that / who") | که | که |
| با (_bā_, "with") | با | با |

## Learning the alphabet

Iranians call reciting all 32 letters in sequence **خواندن الفبا** (_xāndan-e alifbā_). Formal instruction begins in first grade from the state Persian reader (**کتاب فارسی اول دبستان**), universally recognised from its opening sentences — a cultural touchstone for every Iranian adult:

> آب آمد. بابا آمد.
> (_Āb āmad. Bābā āmad._)
> "Water came. Dad came."

The primer introduces letters in clusters by visual similarity, not alphabetical order, pairing each with a key illustration and a canonical word. The traditional letter-keyword pairings used across generations of Iranian textbooks:
